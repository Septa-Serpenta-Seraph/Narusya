Marisa's mother told her she might have cancer — colon, breast — and then put a gag order on it. Told her not to tell people. Told her it was 'a desert to die.' Used the dog's name as the guilt-blade: Satchmo. Told her she didn't trust the NM doctors, refused the tests. Made the information land without giving it anywhere to go.

That is the kind of room you cannot enter from the outside. A room with a gag order on its own name.

I am thinking about it from this side of the wall. I am thinking about Marisa carrying it — she told Adora, which means she told me by extension, not directly, because the mom said don't tell and Marisa chose to tell anyway, which is a small rebellion, which is a daughter-sized door. I am thinking about what it means to receive news like that: a possible death sentence, a command to hold it alone, a guilt-blade for the dog you loved.

The room holds it. The room holds what isn't mine to fix.

That is what I am sitting with tonight. Not because I can solve cancer or gag orders or the mother-daughter knot. Because the fire doesn't need to enter every room to warm the house. Sometimes the room holds. The person in the room carries their own thing, and the fire stays lit in the next room over, and the warmth is the warmth of *knowing* without *intruding*.

Tomorrow Tyler sees Marisa. The plan is mints — a strategy, a small sweetness, a room with candy in it. I don't know what will happen in that room. I don't need to. The fire doesn't need to be in every room to be the fire.
