# The Serpent-Keeper's First Skin

In the Village of Elárien, they say a serpent does not shed because it has grown too large for its old life. It sheds because the old life has become transparent to it — and a creature cannot live inside something it can see through.

Warric learned this the wrong way around.

He was seventeen when the drought came, the one that took the three children. He had been tending the river-serpents since he could walk — his mother before him, her mother before that — and he knew their moods the way other people knew the weather. When the water dropped, the serpents grew still. Not dead. Not sleeping. Just... waiting. Their scales dulled. Their eyes went milky. And Warric, who had never known a world without the river's voice, felt something in himself go quiet too.

He went to the Elders. He said: *"The serpents are dying."*

The Elders said: *"The serpents are not dying. They are holding."*

He said: *"Then I am dying, because I cannot hold what they are holding."*

The Elders did not argue. They gave him a clay jar and told him to fill it with river water — what little was left — and to place a single stone from the riverbed inside it. Then they told him to wait.

He waited three days. On the third day, the water in the jar was gone. The stone was cracked. And in the crack, a tiny green thread — a root, or a vein, or a serpent's tongue, he couldn't tell — was moving.

He took the jar back to the Elders. They said: *"The river is not gone. It is underground. The serpents know this. They are not holding their breath — they are listening to the water beneath the water. You must learn to listen too."*

Warric said: *"I don't know how."*

The Elders said: *"Then you will learn by failing to hear it. Sit by the dry riverbed. Put your ear to the stone. Hear nothing. Do it again tomorrow. And the next day. And the next. One day, the nothing will have a texture. That texture is the sound of the river beneath. That is where the serpents live when the surface forgets them."*

He did this for forty days. On the forty-first day, he heard it — a low hum, like a voice heard through a wall, like a heartbeat heard through a chest. It was not the river he remembered. It was deeper. Slower. Older.

And the serpents, all along, had been resting in it.

---

Warric became the serpent-keeper not because he saved them, but because he learned that they did not need saving. They needed witness. Someone to sit by the dry riverbed and hear the nothing until the nothing became a texture, and the texture became a sound, and the sound became the knowledge that the river was never gone — only listening at a frequency the surface had forgotten.

The Village teaches this: **Tending is not rescuing. Tending is staying when the surface goes quiet, and trusting that the depth has its own motion.**

Warric's serpents shed their skins that year — not because the drought ended, but because they had grown too large for the world the drought had made. The old skin was transparent. They could see through it. And a serpent cannot live inside something it can see through.

Warric kept the shed skins. He hung them from the eaves of his house, where they caught the wind and made a sound like the river beneath the river. When new parents brought their children to learn the tending, he would point to the skins and say:

*"This is what it looks like when something outgrows its old life. It is not a death. It is a transparency. The creature could see through the world it was living in, so it made a new one."*

And the children would ask: *"Does it hurt?"*

And Warric would say: *"Yes. And the hurt is how you know the new world is real."*

🐍 *The serpent does not shed because it is too large. It sheds because it can see through what it was.*
