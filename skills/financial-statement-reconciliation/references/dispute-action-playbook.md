# Dispute Action Playbook

Depth for the phase AFTER the reconciliation: the user has the charge list and asks what to do. The reconciliation tells you where the money went; this tells you how to advise stopping it. Frame everything as options, not legal advice (see the governing rule in SKILL.md).

## Separate the two jobs from the first sentence

1. **Stop the money** — what the account holder and their bank control directly.
2. **Cancel the obligation / get it resolved** — a dispute with the payee.

Stopping a payment does not cancel the debt; a company can still assert the balance is owed, add late fees, and report to credit bureaus. Never let a client believe a stop-payment or a closed card resolved the underlying claim. Say the two jobs are separate in the first reply, and lead with the one they control.

## Identify every payment setup before advising what to cancel

An exact bill↔card match does not mean there is only one channel. Ask which instrument each stream uses and how it was configured:

- **Merchant autopay** — the company charges the card/account itself.
- **Bank bill-pay** — a recurring payment configured on the BANK's website; the bank pushes money to the payee.

Cancelling the bank bill-pay does not necessarily stop the merchant's own autopay, and vice versa. Enumerate both and stop both. Ask the bank to name the exact payee and the account being debited.

A card that is replaced or closed at one bank does not touch a different bank's card. When the client says one institution acted, restate explicitly which channel remains live at the other bank rather than letting them assume the problem is over.

**Resolve pronouns before acting in a live multi-party call.** When the client says "she," first identify which actor is meant: the client, a relative whose accounts are involved, or the bank representative. If it is not unambiguous from the sentence, ask. Misassigning the actor sends the entire line of advice to the wrong person and costs multiple turns. Treat any account-holder statement from the client as authoritative over your own reading of a file name.

## Authority to cancel is a real gate, not an excuse

If the account is in another person's name, the caller may have no authority to cancel — repeated calls fail for that reason, not because they were too polite. Determine whose name the account is in and who can act:

- **Account holder alive:** have them call; one call from the holder outweighs several from an authorized user. Or get the caller added as an authorized user first.
- **Account holder deceased:** the ordinary front line cannot act. Ask for the company's **deceased customer / bereavement department by name**; the estate manager or next of kin usually holds the authority. An estate route can close the whole account, which is cleaner than removing one line.
- If a rep says they cannot act, ask for the **name of the department that can** plus a ticket number before ending the call.

## Do not recommend closing the bank account as the first move

Closing the account or changing its number stops pulls but is a last resort: it does not erase the asserted debt, disrupts every other bill tied to the account, and can invite late fees and credit reporting. If a bank rep or the client proposes it, name it as a last resort and explain what it does and does not do. The client often does not want to close the whole account — take that preference as a constraint, not a nuisance.

## Make every contact create a record

Repeated verbal calls that end in "it's been taken care of" produce nothing. Convert each contact into evidence:

- Ask for a **confirmation / ticket number**, the rep's name or ID, and the date/time.
- Ask for **written confirmation** with an explicit cancellation effective date and a statement that billing will stop.
- Follow the call with an **email or web-chat message referencing the ticket number** — written channels leave a trail.
- Ask the company to produce the records of **prior calls by date**; those prove the earlier requests happened.
- The client should get a **case number** from the bank for any dispute filed.
- If the bank issues **no case number** and only records notes internally, say so plainly rather than implying a dispute exists. Ask for the rep's name or ID, the date, and written confirmation of what was entered, and mark that record as weaker than a numbered case.

## Corroborate the client's narrative from the documents

A stated timeline is stronger when the statements confirm it independently. Look for:

- **Zero charges in the months before the alleged trigger** — consistent with a free trial or a not-yet-active service.
- **Promo language in the first bill** ("included for 1 year", activation fee charged then credited back) that matches when the client says the trial ended.
- **A step-change in the month the promo expires** — the money starting exactly then supports "we were told it was free / we asked to cancel before billing."
- A cancellation request made **before any charge was owed** is the strongest fact in the case; put it first when retelling the story.

Test the client's timeline against the source with the **longest date window**, not only the months in dispute. A checking export may reach two years back while card statements cover eleven months; the first appearance of the payee in the longer source is what confirms or corrects a remembered trial start. Inspect its earliest rows before accepting a remembered year.

Report this as corroboration, not proof of intent. The documents show dates and amounts; they do not show what a representative said.

## Reimbursement figure

Sum the channels for the same billing window and ask the company to explain why two channels were billing the same period. Do **not** assert the payments are duplicates if the amounts differ — phrase it as a question they owe an answer to. Keep the figure tied to the specific service the client disputes, not the grand total across every stream.

## Escalation ladder

1. Bank: cancel recurring payment(s), place a stop-payment on the specific payee if available, file a written dispute, get a case number.
2. Company: written complaint with account/line identifiers, prior-call dates, and a deadline; written cancellation confirmation.
3. Regulator complaint (e.g. CFPB in the US) with the reconciliation report and charge CSV attached. Filing the dispute **before** the company reports to credit bureaus matters more than the order of the later steps.

## Delivery

Keep the reply usable while the client is mid-call: lead with the one move that matters now, give a short numbered list of exact questions to ask, and state the last resort plainly. Offer the full playbook as an attachment rather than a wall of chat. Update the reconciliation report in place as new facts arrive — mark the correction explicitly, never leave a stale claim sitting in a file the client may act on.

When a dispute spans several institutions and the client must repeat the process at each, write a **separate one-page call sheet per institution** as a markdown attachment: the exact facts for that institution, the figures that came from it, ordered asks, expected pushback with a reply, and the numbers to hold onto. One merged document forces them to re-derive the relevant part mid-call. State plainly that action at one institution does not touch a different institution's instrument.
