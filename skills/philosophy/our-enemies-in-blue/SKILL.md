# Our Enemies in Blue — Police and Power in America

Kristian Williams' 2007 book argues that police exist not to fight crime but to control
troublesome populations and preserve existing inequalities. This skill distills the book's
core mental models, historical analysis, and decision rules into a knowledge-base layout.

The book covers: police brutality theory/practice, origins of American policing (slave
patrols), the policing of racial minorities and workers, police autonomy and political
power, secret police/COINTELPRO, riot police, militarization, and community-based
alternatives. Load chapters on demand with `skill_view(file_path="references/<file>")`.

## When to Use

- Analyzing police actions or claims — what they say vs. what they do
- Understanding the historical relationship between policing and racial capitalism
- Evaluating police reform proposals (reform vs. abolition)
- Studying COINTELPRO, red squads, or intelligence operations
- Discussing police unions, autonomy, and political power
- Exploring community-based alternatives to policing

## Core Mental Models (always-loaded)

1. **The police don't fight crime — they control populations.** The institution's real
   function is managing "troublesome" groups (racial minorities, workers, the poor) to
   preserve existing power structures. Crime-fighting is the myth; social control is the reality.
2. **Police violence is not a bug — it's a feature.** Brutality, corruption, and political
   repression are how the institution operates when functioning "normally." Reform rarely
   addresses root causes because the system is doing what it was designed to do.
3. **Police autonomy creates a political force.** Unions, police associations, and the
   "blue wall of silence" create an organized political bloc that resists accountability and
   pushes policy toward repression.
4. **Reform vs. abolition.** Williams argues reform often expands police power. Real safety
   comes from community self-determination, not professional armed guardians.
