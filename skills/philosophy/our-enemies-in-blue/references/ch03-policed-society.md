# Chapter 3: The Genesis of a Policed Society

## Core Argument

The modern police institution emerged from the intersection of political machines, moral panics about urbanization, and the need to control "dangerous classes" (the poor, immigrants, racial minorities). It was not a response to crime but a tool for imposing order on populations that threatened existing power structures.

## Political Machines and Police

- **Edward C. Banfield & James Q. Wilson**: City Politics — political machines (Tammany Hall, etc.) exchanged material inducements for political loyalty; police departments were key levers of machine power
- **Raymond B. Fosdick**: American Police Systems — documented the experimentation and instability in American police organization; change rather than stability marked its course
- **Charles Tilly**: "War Making and State Making as Organized Crime" — government racketeering; the state as protection racket
- Police became closely tied to both the existing structure of primary justice and the new structure of urban politics

## The Demand for Order

- **Allan Silver**: "The Demand for Order in Civil Society" — bourgeois perceptions of urban problems led to political initiative to control "dangerous classes"
- **Roger Lane**: Policing the City — cities had a "civilizing" effect, but the police were needed to manage those who resisted civilization
- **Michael Stephen Hindus**: Prison and Plantation — class-control function of criminal prosecutions; defendants from "deviant" classes lost their individuality
- **Max Weber**: The Protestant Ethic and the Spirit of Capitalism — religious justification for controlling the habits of the working class
- **Christine Stansell**: City of Women — altered bourgeois perceptions made visible and subject to intervention areas of social life previously taken for granted

## The Police as Social Control

- Police provided overnight lodging for the homeless, distributed free firewood and shoes, ran soup kitchens and employment services — but this charity was also control
- "Assembly-line justice" — tendency toward efficiency, ruthlessness, and railroading; defendants seen as interchangeable members of a deviant class
- Public order crimes = working-class leisure-time activity
- "It takes real cops to make drunk arrests" — policing targets the moral failures of the poor

## Key Themes

1. **Police as a response to urbanization**: not to crime but to the perceived disorder of the city
2. **Class control**: police target the poor, immigrants, and racial minorities
3. **Moral regulation**: police enforce bourgeois standards of behavior (sobriety, orderliness)
4. **Institutional continuity**: the function (control) remains even as the form changes

## Decision Rules

- When analyzing a policing practice, ask: whose order is being maintained? Whose behavior is being regulated?
- When evaluating "reform": does it change the function or just the form?
- When police provide "services" (charity, aid), recognize this as also a form of control
