# Chapter 7: Secret Police, Red Squads, and the Strategy of Permanent Repression

## Core Argument

Secret police, red squads, and intelligence operations are a core function of modern policing. They target political dissidents, infiltrate organizations, and use surveillance and disruption to maintain the status quo. This is not a deviation from normal policing — it is an integral part of it.

## The Haymarket Affair

- **Paul Avrich**: The Haymarket Tragedy — the 1886 Haymarket affair as a case study in anti-union repression
- **Henry David**: The History of the Haymarket Affair — the trial and execution of anarchists
- **Bruce C. Nelson**: Beyond the Martyrs — social history of Chicago's anarchists
- **Joseph G. Rayback**: A History of American Labor — the Knights of Labor and the aftermath

## Red Squads and Repression

- **Frank Donner**: Protectors of Privilege — the single best history of red squads and repression in urban America
- **Ward Churchill & Jim Vander Wall**: Agents of Repression — the FBI's secret wars against the Black Panther Party and American Indian Movement
- **Church Committee (1976)**: Senate Select Committee to Study Government Operations with Respect to Intelligence Activities — documented the extent of government surveillance and disruption

## COINTELPRO

- **Church Committee**: Final Report — FBI's COINTELPRO program targeted domestic dissent
- **Ward Churchill & Jim Vander Wall**: The COINTELPRO Papers — documents from the FBI's secret wars
- **J. Edgar Hoover**: instructed agents that "Negro youth and moderates must be made to understand that if they succumb to revolutionary teaching, they will be dead revolutionaries"
- **Black Panther Party**: FBI used forged letters, spread rumors, and exacerbated internal tensions to destroy the party
- **Geronimo Pratt**: BPP member framed and imprisoned for 27 years; the Panthers' internal divisions (exacerbated by FBI) undermined his defense

## The Strategy of Permanent Repression

- Police gathered information from informers planted by private agencies, employers' associations, and patriotic groups
- By the 1930s, big-city police recruited their own informers
- In the 1960s, police went underground — undercover agents became commonplace
- **Alan Wolfe**: The Seamy Side of Democracy — repression in America
- **Frank Kitson**: Low Intensity Operations — subversion, insurgency, peace-keeping

## Decision Rules

- When analyzing police intelligence operations, recognize them as a core function, not an aberration
- When studying social movements, look for evidence of infiltration and disruption
- When evaluating government surveillance, ask: who is targeted and why?
- When a movement declines, consider whether state repression (not just internal factors) contributed
